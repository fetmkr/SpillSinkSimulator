Two structures in series: which layer owns which axis

Phase 2 ended with no single geometry good at all three jobs. The bought honeycomb swallows the most light and is last at destroying shape and last at dimming the view. The cone owns both of those and is mid-table on total. So: stack them, and see whether two scales in series beat one. The envelope stays at 50 mm and is split between the layers, so nothing here wins by being twice as thick.

This page was rebuilt on 2026-08-14 after a geometry defect was found by eye in its own gallery: the commercial-honeycomb builder stepped its lattice along the wrong two axes, so no cell shared an edge with any neighbour and 30.1 % of the panel face was open channel straight down to the flat backing slab. Every honeycomb and every stacked number was re-measured on geometry that tessellates. One headline reversed; the two findings below survived, and one of them gained a control it did not have.

A prediction was written before the render, and it was half wrong — which is the useful half. The stack module's docstring predicted that only the top layer is exposed head-on, so honeycomb-over-cone should inherit the honeycomb's head-on failure. Form behaved exactly that way. Head-on did not: it improved {{HC_GAIN}}-fold. So the honeycomb's head-on brightness never came from its wall tops. It came from the flat backing slab at the bottom of every cell, seen straight down the tube.

The control that settles it, and it only exists because of the rebuild. Honeycomb over a finer honeycomb keeps the tube and keeps a flat floor at the end of it, and reads {{CC_PEAK}} head-on — identical to honeycomb alone ({{HC_PEAK}}). Honeycomb over cones keeps the same tube and replaces only the floor, and reads {{HCCONE_PEAK}}. Same top layer in all three, same cell mouth, same wall tops; the only thing that moved is what sits at the bottom, and it moved the number {{HC_GAIN}}×. The explanation published in the phase 2 report — that the flat wall tops face the viewer like a flat plate — is wrong.

The honeycomb underneath does not recover what the shortened cone lets out. Running the same split the other way barely moves at all — honeycomb on top stays inside {{COMBTOP_RANGE}}% across all three splits — because a honeycomb is a light trap, and a trap already deep enough gains nothing from more depth. For a cone, depth inside one continuous cavity is not interchangeable with the same depth divided between two. That is why every mixed stack loses, and why the one stack that wins is two honeycombs: neither layer had depth it could not spare.

What to do with this

If a panel has to be good at more than one thing

There is still no design that leads all three. Cones over honeycomb takes head-on outright — {{CONECOMB_PEAK}}, the lowest anywhere in the study, with {{CONECOMB_SMEAR}}× smear — and pays {{CONECOMB_TOTAL}}% against {{CONE_TOTAL}}% for a cone that keeps the whole 50 mm, {{CONECOMB_COST}}% worse and last on this page. Honeycomb over finer honeycomb takes total outright at {{CC_TOTAL}}% and is last on the other two ({{CC_SMEAR}}× smear, {{CC_PEAK}} head-on). The axes still pull against each other; phase 3 only made the pull legible.

And the two designs leading total reflectance cannot be handled. Both use 0.04 mm foil, below the 0.05 mm floor set for a panel that survives being carried and mounted. Applying that floor, the darkest buildable design in the whole study is {{BEST_OK_NAME}} at {{BEST_OK}}% ({{BEST_OK_PROC}}) — a blade array, not a honeycomb and not a stack.

Whether that is a bad trade depends on something nobody has decided: whether the wall is judged by an instrument or by a person. Total reflectance is what an integrating sphere reads. Head-on brightness is what the audience sees. They are different numbers, and this phase pulled them {{HC_GAIN}}× apart on one geometry — so the question has to be answered before a panel is chosen, not after.

On the evidence here the honest recommendation is still not a stack. It is the single cone layer: {{CONE_PEAK}} head-on and {{CONE_SMEAR}}× smear at {{CONE_TOTAL}}%, buildable at a 0.4 mm moulded tip, better than every stack on two axes at once. What phase 3 bought is not a design — it is the explanation, and the explanation says where the next effort goes: the floor of the cavity, not its walls. Nothing measured so far rules out keeping a full-depth 50 mm honeycomb and shaping only the last 2–3 mm of each cell, which would take the honeycomb's total and the cone's head-on at the same time. That is phase 4.

Nothing here has been built or measured. Two coating properties that were chosen rather than measured — the diffuse fraction and the specular roughness — set the absolute level of every number on this page. One printed coupon on a goniometer settles both, and would also test the mechanism this phase just corrected.
