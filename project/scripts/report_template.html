Nine ways to stop a wall from repeating the artwork

Hundreds of laser projectors converge in mid-air to form an image. Every beam continues past it and lands on a wall, which paints a sharp, legible copy of the artwork. This is a search for a panel surface that sends as little of that back as possible — run across nine geometry families, none of which is the V-groove or the cone the project had been building.

The mechanism

The two coating models want opposite tilts

Under a purely specular coating an inclined plate redirects the bounce, and steeper is better all the way to about 12°. Under a purely diffuse one there is no bounce to redirect — every square millimetre of wall has a direct view of the sky and simply leaks — so shallow is better. The design that survives both is set by where the curves cross, not by either one's own optimum.

This replaced an earlier wrong conclusion. The first pass reported that shallower tilt was monotonically better and recommended pushing below 5°. The geometry builder was clamping tilt to a 5° floor, so tilts of 1°, 2° and 3° all built the same shape and returned identical numbers to five decimal places. Those 450 rows were voided and re-measured.

What died

Exposed area no longer predicts anything

Every design decision in this project until now rested on one rule: the head-on return is the exposed tip area and essentially nothing else. It was verified across 24 combinations — under the old, Fresnel-free material model. Against the fitted coating it does not hold. Across these nine families the exposed area spans {{AREA_SPAN}}× while the score spans only {{SCORE_SPAN}}×, and they do not even rank in the same order.

The nested cell exposes 21.3% of its area and scores within 3.5% of the cone, which exposes 1.0%. The strut lattice exposes 8.6% and is the worst thing here. If area still drove the answer, neither could be true. “Shrink the tip” is no longer the primary design move — and that argument was the whole reason this project moved from V-grooves to cones.

Corrections

Four defects found during this run

Recorded because each one first presented as an interesting result. Three were caught by looking at a picture or at a number that was too round; one was found by a check built specifically to try to falsify a claim.

The honeycomb was not a honeycomb

Hexagons were drawn around jittered centres, but a hexagon only tiles the plane on an exact lattice. The cells stopped sharing edges: walls doubled up, gaps opened between them, and the wall-sharing de-duplication was silently defeated.

Caught in the 3D render, invisible in the CSV. Rebuilt as a Voronoi tessellation of the same points.

The flat control plate was embedded inside the panel

The control sits 100 mm to the side, but the panel field runs 255 mm. The control has been standing inside the geometry it is supposed to be compared against, for every 3D family, from the beginning.

Invisible while cone tips covered 0.26% of it; a wall network covering 13% made it read 0.0423 instead of 0.0500. Absolute reflectances were measured to be unaffected; ratios against the control are not.

A 5° clamp ate an entire experiment

Tilts of 1°, 2° and 3° all clamped to 5° and returned bit-identical results, which read as a plateau and produced a wrong recommendation to push the tilt lower.

The give-away was three different inputs agreeing to five decimal places. 450 rows voided and re-run.

Leaning cells cannot lean independently

Two neighbouring cells cannot tilt their shared wall in two different directions. The first attempt dropped 77% of the walls. Displacing the Voronoi seed points with depth along a smooth direction field keeps the tessellation exact; where adjacency still changes, the wall is built anyway rather than skipped.

Overlap is absorbed by the union; a gap is not. That asymmetry was learned from an earlier bug that presented as “regular arrays are 8× darker”.

What this does not settle

open itemwhy it matters
The coating's diffuse fraction Unmeasured, and it moves designs by up to 41× and inverts the ranking. Every score here is the worse of two extremes precisely because of it. Only a printed and measured coupon settles it.
Form, not just brightness The stated first priority is destroying the shape of what returns, so the wall reads as texture rather than as letters. This run ranks on total reflectance only. The form metric that folds brightness and blur together is defined but not yet trustworthy.
Printability of the winner A 0.05 mm knife edge is an eighth of one nozzle width. The rank-7 design uses 0.02 mm. These are optics probes, not buildable parts; the printable edge is 0.4 mm and has been measured in the same series.
Depth 80 mm Inside the 30–100 mm envelope, but at the deep end, and depth 100 was measured slightly worse at one pitch — so the depth optimum is real rather than “more is better”.
