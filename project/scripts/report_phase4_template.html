The last three millimetres

Phase 3 found that a honeycomb panel is bright head-on for one reason: every cell is a tube pointing at the viewer with a flat slab at the end of it. It fixed that by spending half the 50 mm envelope on a cone field, which worked and cost total reflectance. Phase 4 asks the obvious next question — how little of the envelope does the floor actually need? The answer is two or three millimetres out of fifty, and the best floor is the cheapest one to make.

the control that matters
Air gap does nothing

Move the slab back 3 mm and leave it flat: {{GAP_TH0}}%, or {{GAP_GAIN}}× — no change at all. Distance is not the mechanism. A ray that meets a flat surface square-on comes straight back whether it travelled 47 mm or 50 to get there.

The prediction was written before the render and it held. sweep_floor.py recorded three expectations: head-on collapses at the shallowest floor, worst-case total barely moves, and form does not improve at all. All three came out. The one that would have falsified phase 3's mechanism — head-on scaling with floor depth, meaning the floor was just a short cone — did not happen.

  • form destruction {{SMEAR_bl050o115_pyramid}}×
  • head-on brightness {{PEAK_bl050o115_pyramid}} against {{PEAK_bl050o115_flat}} on a flat floor

A phase 2 note is confirmed, not overturned. sweep_topo.py recorded that blade overlap 1.15 beat 1.45 everywhere, measured at 30 mm depth. An accidental run here at 1.45 looked 1.65× darker and appeared to contradict it — but that compared two different sweeps. Measured side by side at 50 mm, 1.15 wins on all four numbers: 0.1861% against 0.1887% worst-case, 0.05383% against 0.05713% head-on, 3.96× against 3.54× on form, 0.092 against 0.098 to the eye. The wider blade is not better; the earlier comparison was.

Phase 3 proved form destruction is set by the layer the light meets first. A honeycomb tube smears a returned line by {{SMEAR_p650f080_flat}}× — that is, not at all — and no floor can ever fix that, because the floor is the last thing the light meets, not the first. So the bought track can win two axes and never the third. If a legible reflection of the artwork is unacceptable, the blade array is the only track on the table, and phase 4's contribution there is fixing its head-on brightness for the price of a pressed sheet.

Reflectance at normal incidence, worst case over the three coating models, averaged over three geometry seeds. This is the axis a floor exists to move.

This is not head-on brightness, and the heading used to say it was. Total reflectance counts every photon that comes back; head-on brightness counts only the ones aimed at an eye. They can disagree by a factor of twenty-four: a cone array returns 0.180 % at normal incidence — about what a flat-floored honeycomb returns — while its head-on peak is 0.068 against the honeycomb's 1.634, because the cone spreads what it returns. Gallery 5 is the one a viewer experiences.

The one real trade-off

Blade tilt splits the three axes against each other

Everything else on this page was free: the floor moved normal-incidence reflectance and left worst-case total and form untouched, its pitch barely mattered, its apex flat cost 1.4 % at a manufacturable 0.1 mm. Tilt is different. On the leading design — 0.05 mm blades, 5.5 mm slotted grid, 47 mm, over a 3 mm pyramid floor — the three axes want three different answers.

Upright blades are the darkest panel in the study and destroy nothing. At 0° the blades form parallel slots; light rattles up and down between them and leaves the way it came. 1.13× smear is the honeycomb's failure reached from the other direction — dark, and a legible reflection. A design chosen on total reflectance alone lands exactly here, which is what makes the three-axis scoring worth its cost.

So the recommendation does not move: 2° leads total by 13 % and gives up 1 % of form. But if what the client judges is the view rather than an instrument, 5° is the better buy — 1.7× dimmer to the eye, marginally better on form, 13 % worse on total.

The same two unmeasured coating properties still set the absolute level of every number here. The diffuse fraction and the specular roughness were chosen, not measured, and phase 2 showed the head-on figure moving 332× across the plausible roughness range. Phase 4's ratios are robust — every design on this page shares one coating, so a wrong coating moves them all together — but the absolute percentages are not. One printed coupon on a goniometer closes that, and it is now the only thing between this study and a specification.
