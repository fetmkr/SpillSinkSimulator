The Wall That Eats Light

There is a room where hundreds of laser projectors draw pictures in mid-air. The light that paints the picture keeps flying, and lands on a wall. If the wall gives that light back, the audience sees graffiti instead of art. This is the record of reinventing that wall: nine phases of research carried out daily over more than two months, over 2,500 candidate designs and tens of thousands of measurements, ten major mistakes caught by our own checks, and the simulator that now holds it all.

Prologue

The problem — the wall copies the art

First, picture what actually happens.

Fill a venue with thin haze and converge hundreds of laser projectors on one spot. Where the beams cross, letters and figures appear in the air — visible only because the haze particles scatter a small share of each beam toward your eyes.

The trouble starts afterwards. A beam does not stop where it drew. Nearly all of its power keeps flying and eventually strikes a wall. The picture in the air glows on a few scattered percent; the wall receives the full beam. So the wall carries a copy of the artwork far sharper than the artwork itself, and the audience reads the wall.

The beams cannot be blanked — the beam in flight is the piece. One answer remains: the wall has to eat the light.

The real brush is 7–14 mm wide at the wall (a LaserCube Ultra 7.5 W MK2: 4 mm aperture, 1 mrad divergence — about 7 mm at 3 m, 14 mm at 10 m). That width will matter in several verdicts below.

HOW TO READ THIS REPORT

Every act follows the same frame: what we asked → what we tried → what we learned → what we decided. Unfamiliar words are collected in the glossary at the end. And one habit to know in advance: we wrote our predictions down before running each measurement, and graded them honestly afterwards. That is why the sentence "we were wrong" appears so often here. Those sentences are the most valuable part of the study.

The Ruler

Build the ruler first — three axes

To choose a good wall, you first need a ruler that measures "good."

Our first naive measurement failed loudly: shining a beam head-on and reading with a camera in the same place gave a ratio of 46,000 at one angle and nearly zero at the next. A ruler that jumps like that is not a ruler.

So we rebuilt the instrument. Light the whole sky (a uniform hemisphere) and read the panel with a tilted camera. Because light travels the same path in both directions, that reading equals "everything that returns when a beam enters at that angle, no matter where it exits." The instrument passed its own exams: a zero-reflectance panel reads exactly zero — the ruler has no noise floor — and a 5% reference plate reads 5.0000% at every angle.

And we scored on three axes, not one, because one number always lies. A commercial aluminium honeycomb cuts total reflection five-fold while behaving exactly like a bare flat plate on the other two axes. Score one axis and you buy the wrong wall.

Axis 1 · Total return
How much comes back
The share of incoming light returned to the room, quoted at the worst material and worst angle.
Axis 2 · Shape destruction
Does the picture survive
Whether the returned light keeps its line shape or blurs into texture. A ratio against a flat plate of the same paint — higher is better.
Axis 3 · Head-on glint
Does it flash the eye
The brightest spot fired straight back at the source, with a flat matte plate as 1. Lower is better.

We added a discipline of reproducibility on top. The same design, re-measured months apart, must return the same value to the last digit. One reference design reads 0.215484% in six different measurement files across the whole study, without a single last-digit difference. That discipline is what later let us catch the biggest bug of the project.

Act 1 · The Beginning

Act 1 · The era of grooves

The first candidates were simple: cut deep grooves into a plate.

We tried five families of cross-sections extruded along one axis — tilted slats, open troughs, deep sharp V-grooves, folded labyrinths, and serrated flanks. Several laws measured here still stand: depth divided by pitch above 2 is dark within ±40°, above 6 is dark at every angle; and the light that comes back is dominated not by many-bounce survivors but by light that hit once and left.

The best V-groove reached one two-hundredth of a flat plate head-on. By the numbers, a success. But the second axis failed: a line drawn parallel to the grooves came back as a line. A profile extruded along one axis has no structure along that axis, so its shape-destruction score was near zero at every angle.

DECISION

The first-priority mission — destroying the picture — cannot be done with grooves. Go three-dimensional.

Act 2

Act 2 · Going 3D, and three traps

The cone field was strong from day one — and fooled us three times on day one.

Trap one: the 27% incident. The first 3D measurement returned a reflectance of 27% — 55 times the paint's own reflectance, physically impossible. The culprit was the measurement margin: a tilted camera looks past the edge of the panel, and it had been reading the background world behind the geometry. Every steep-angle number before that day was discarded.

Trap two: the 8× illusion. "Regular arrays are 8× darker than jittered ones" looked like physics. It was gaps: jittered cones stopped meeting at their bases and the backing plate showed through, inflating brightness 6.7×. Closing the gaps withdrew an entire conclusion.

Trap three: the 66× glint. One panel flashed 66× at a single angle. Its rounded tips had been approximated by six flat facets whose mirror normals lined up at exactly that angle. Finer curvature made it vanish.

Even with the traps cleared, one more lesson remained. Our first "3D beats 1D five-fold" comparison had mismatched tip conditions by a factor of 24. Matched fairly, the gap became 2.9× — still a win, but the honest one.

Act 3 · The Turning Night

Act 3 · The night the paint model was wrong

Choose structures perfectly, and it is all worthless if you are wrong about the paint. That happened.

Our black-paint model assumed the same reflectance at every angle. A precise laboratory measurement of actual Musou Black (Reference 1) says otherwise: 1% head-on rising to 3.2% at grazing angles. We had been 2× optimistic head-on, 7× optimistic at grazing — and the angular trend ran in the wrong direction.

Swapping in the measured curve turned the world over. The flat plate got 2× worse, the cones up to 41× worse, and the ranking inverted — the design in first place fell to last. No past comparison could be rescaled; everything was re-measured.

WHAT THAT NIGHT LEFT BEHIND

The same night we built the locks and the gate checks. Every published number is now automatically compared against its source file, and the report generator that still contained two withdrawn claims was disarmed. A number that cannot prove itself does not appear in a report.

Act 4 · Phase 2

Act 4 · The structure zoo

Next question: take every structure in the world, build each at the size its own factory can deliver — who wins?

Nine structural families entered one arena, each at its honest process limit: bought honeycomb at supplier-catalogue cell and foil sizes, sheet-metal shingles at 0.1 mm steel, moulded cones at a 0.4 mm tip. 180 designs, roughly twelve thousand measurements.

And the darkness leader — the nested cell — was disqualified here. Its numbers were flawless. The lesson entered the project's vocabulary — an optimiser always walks to the edge of whatever box you give it — and every winner since has been rendered and looked at first.

Act 5 · Phase 3–4

Act 5 · Stacks, and the law of the floor

Stack two layers, keep both virtues? The experiment taught something simpler.

We stacked a shape-destroying layer over a glint-killing layer, in every order and split. Two laws emerged, with no exceptions across every pair: the top layer sets the shape score; the bottom layer sets the head-on glint.

Those laws exposed the honeycomb's true culprit. Its head-on failure was never the wall tops — a honeycomb cell is a tube aimed at the viewer with a flat backing plate at the far end. Replace only that floor, and 92% of the glint disappears.

How much of the depth must be shaped? Astonishingly little. Pressing only the last 2–3 mm of a 50 mm cell into pyramids buys a five-fold improvement. A 3 mm air gap in the same place — the control — buys nothing. Shape does the work, not distance. And the flat spot on a pyramid tip pays back linearly in area: a 0.1 mm tip is effectively free; 0.4 mm costs 18%.

This act had its own incident. The honeycomb-building code had stitched its hexagons wrong, leaving 30% of the panel as open channels straight down to the backing. The CSV looked fine; the render passed at a glance. A human eye browsing the report gallery caught it. After the rebuild one conclusion flipped, and "honeycombs must tessellate" became an assertion the code enforces on itself.

Act 6 · The Mid-Project Turn

Act 6 · The day everything was inside-out

The largest single correction of the study: three structural families had been built inside-out from the start.

It began as a small inconsistency — a shell with no holes must measure the same as the solid it replaces, and it was off by 34%. Seven plausible explanations were executed one by one: coincident faces, funnel orientation, micro-slits, double walls, floating-point joints, the GPU, light reaching the interior. All acquitted. What remained was face orientation. Every face of a 3D model has an inside and an outside, and three families — cone, shingle, pyramid — had been built with their skins pointing inward. The renderer was innocent; we had dressed the geometry inside-out and nearly blamed the mirror.

Act 7 · Phase 5

Act 7 · The pyramid, alone

The champion was chosen. Thirteen sub-phases then attacked it from every side.

Should it be jittered? — No

The fear of regular arrays came from Act 1's grooves, whose brightness swings 214× with brush position. A pyramid field swings 1.01×. A cell closed on all four sides is uniform by itself; jittering the apexes cost up to 24% for nothing. Decision: build it periodic. One die.

How deep? — Steepness is everything

Sweeping depth and pitch separately collapsed onto one curve: depth divided by pitch. Steeper is darker, and the curve was still falling at the end of the tested range. Where to stop became a tooling-cost question, not an optical one.

Copy the RF chamber? — A trap

RF anechoic chambers mix large and small pyramids, and the photos make you want to copy them. But that trick widens a wavelength band — a wave mechanism. We live in ray optics, where performance follows steepness, so a mixed field is just a mixture of steepnesses: strictly worse. Measured: 28–40% worse, exactly as predicted. Never copy a pattern because the photograph is impressive.

The brush width changes the verdict

Shape-destruction scores turned out to hinge on brush-to-cell size. A 10 mm-pitch pyramid is excellent on totals but returns a line narrower than a flat wall's under the real 7–14 mm brush. To shred a line, the cells must be finer than the brush. Beam width became a first-class input — in the simulator too.

The axis nobody had measured — the compass hole

Until this point every measurement ran in the panel's frontal plane. Rotating the panel exposed a hole: light approaching from about 30° around the compass reads 74% brighter. The definition of "worst case" changed that day, and the duel against the rotation-symmetric cone ended — by the verdict rule registered in advance — in a genuine draw. Reality broke the tie: the cone's advantage (smear) evaporates at real brush widths, while the pyramid's advantage (head-on glint) survives every brush width and every compass direction.

Held hostage by the paint

The last sub-phase taught humility. Depending on the paint's surface roughness — a value never yet measured on a physical coupon — the honest "N× darker than flat" claim moves between 3 and 11.6. The design choice is robust; the marketing number is not. So the claim was written as "3–5×" until a real coupon says otherwise.

Act 8 · Phase 6

The final spec — pitch 4 · depth 22 · tip 0.4

The champion was a needle bed at 2 mm pitch — a die-maker's nightmare. So we measured an easy-to-build tier.

The conclusion: pitch 4 mm, depth 20 mm, tip 0.1 mm. Less steep, yet its three-axis scores nearly match the champion, and the tip requirement is four times looser — pressable. This design became the study's reference sample.

The ORDER spec was then adjusted once more. Depth grew from 20 to 22 mm for margin, and the tip relaxed from 0.1 to 0.4 mm to buy die life and machining tolerance. The price is stated honestly: head-on glint rises from 0.040 to 0.173 — still a sixth of a flat matte plate. Total return rises only 4%, and smear actually improves slightly. Two verdicts sealed this design's fate. First, valley fillets are forbidden. A rounded bead of just 0.1 mm radius left in the valleys more than doubles the head-on limit — a concave trough concentrates light back toward its source. That one verdict chose the process: standard injection moulding is rejected (it cannot hold sharp valleys); silicone-mould casting or row-strip assembly can. Second, it gets stronger where walls get grazed. At 70° the flat Musou wall blooms to 4.3% while the final sample sits at 0.198% — a 21.6× advantage. One panel type covers the whole wall, including the corners the projectors graze.

Act 9 · Phase 7

Act 9 · The box lost five times

If there is space behind the wall, shouldn't a recessed box beat a flat panel? We built five. All five lost.

A plain box, zigzag-folded walls, louvre-folded walls, accordion walls, and finally a box clad on every face with our own panel. Built as real geometry and measured, every one lost to a single flat panel — some were brighter than a bare flat plate.

The losses bought three design laws. A concave right-angle corner facing the beam is a retroreflector. No face normal may point into the incoming cone of light. Absorbing texture needs fine, near-vertical faces — and 1 mm sheet metal cannot be folded into that. The conclusion was clean: the best use of space behind a wall is distance — move the panel back. The flat universal panel is the product.

Act 10 · Phase 8

Act 10 · The window — transmit, don't absorb

Directly opposite the projectors, where the beams strike hardest, a different idea was needed: don't eat the light. Pour it away.

Take museum-grade anti-reflective glass (under 1% reflection per surface), hinge it at the top, tilt it 35° back. 99% of the light passes through into a pyramid-lined trap below, and the 1% reflection is always folded downward. The tilted plane's arithmetic guarantees — by proof, not by scan — that no beam from above can ever reflect toward level or upward sightlines. The audience's eyes are safe by construction.

Measurement found one dangerous exception, and watched another disappear. At a 25° tilt there was a single razor-thin retro spike — 34,000× at exactly one angle, gone five degrees away. At 35° the spike left the measured field entirely. The renders then showed a sparkle band at the top edge of the glass, and extending the rim cover over the top quarter erased that too.

Final numbers at audience eye level: 0.005–0.038% — 5× to 35× darker than the pyramid wall itself. The installation rule reduced to one line: keep about one metre of dark floor in front of the unit. Nothing wakes it except a bright object standing in that strip.

Act 11 · Phase 9

Act 11 · Can we make a hundred of these?

The last gate is not optics. It is the factory: what can actually cover a hundred square metres, and at what cost?

Why cheap extrusion fails — the 40× law

The factory's sweetest answer is extruding a groove profile from a roll. Measurement closed that door. A pyramid's tip is a point — 0.06% of the panel's area. A groove's tip is a line — 2.5%. Head-on glint follows tip area, so at equal tip width the line pays 40× more than the point. Measured: pyramid 0.040, groove 0.894. And extrusion dies wear their tips round first, so the line only grows in production. This is the deep reason the pyramid wins.

The 0.18 rule — one number predicts every coating

What if panels are cast in bare black urethane, no paint at all? Whatever the material's reflectance, this geometry returns 0.18 of it. Three bare materials sat exactly on that line, and the measured Musou panel (0.177% from a 1% paint) obeys the same coefficient. Budgeting became arithmetic: the slopes multiply the painted surface tenfold, so 100 m² of wall is 1,000 m² of paint. Full Musou coverage costs 50–100M KRW in paint alone; painting only the audience-critical 10–20% brings it to 5–20M KRW, while the bare zones still sit 33–88× darker than a white wall.

Two shortcuts, measured shut

Spraying paint on the tips only (one sixteenth of the paint bill) buys totals but cannot touch the head-on glint — the two axes weight depth in opposite directions. Totals are earned at the tips; the glint is earned everywhere, mostly at the bottom. Rejected. Flocked fibre sheet fails at every fibre angle we measured: looking straight down between the fibers, the flat floor shines like a bare plate. Rejected. Both "maybes" were closed with numbers, so they exit the quotation honestly.

The production route, settled

PRODUCTION SUMMARY

3D-printed master → platinum silicone moulds (consumables, 30–50 casts each) → foamed urethane casting, about 0.6–1 kg per panel, 3–4 moulds per hundred panels. Standard injection is optically rejected (valley fillets); extrusion is rejected as the default (the 40× law). Two-tier painting: Musou only where the audience looks, carbon-black bare urethane elsewhere. Corners simply butt at right angles — no cove strips, no fillets, no rules.

Verification

Measured with other people's rulers

If every number comes from one renderer, everything collapses the day that renderer is wrong. So we borrowed other rulers.

Verification ran three layers deep. An independent renderer: a completely separate research renderer (Mitsuba) re-measured representative designs and agreed within 4%. Closed-form answers: we built scenes whose answers exist as formulas — an integrating sphere, a canyon — and graded both renderers against them; ours hit the exact answer within 0.3%, the other drifted past 2% on certain structures. A third referee: an independently written plain-Python ray tracer sided with our renderer. Every published number is the audited renderer's.

We also reconciled with the literature. The closest published prior art is a 2025 paper by a Zeiss researcher (Reference 2) reporting "0.65× of a flat plate" where we claim 0.19× — a gap of more than 3×. Reading the full paper and reproducing his conditions resolved it: his specimen's top face is 30.6% flat cap layer, while our panel's flat area is 0.06%. Dial our geometry to his cap fraction and our number rises to 0.33–0.40×. Most of the gap was his cap. The residual 1.4–1.8× is recorded as open, with three named candidate causes.

The last layer is reproducibility. One anchor value, 0.0013391865650191903, reproduces bit for bit every time it is re-measured. A single pair of values from the earliest code disagrees by 0.055%; instead of papering over it, it is registered in a deviation ledger that the gate check consults — silent drift still fails the build.

Honesty

The times we were wrong

The credibility of this study comes not from the predictions we got right but from the mistakes we caught. Here are the ten largest, undisguised.

MISTAKE 01

The 27% incident

The first 3D measurement returned a physically impossible value. The tilted camera was reading the background world past the edge of the panel.

Lesson — a surprising result is usually a bug's first symptom. The margin rule went into the code.

MISTAKE 02

"Jittered arrays are 8× brighter"

It looked like physics. It was daylight: jittered cones stopped meeting at their bases and the backing showed through. Closing the gaps erased 6.7× of it.

Lesson — render the geometry and look at it before publishing a conclusion.

MISTAKE 03

The typed-in baseline

"A flat plate is 6.12%" turned out to be a number someone had typed, traceable to no measurement. Measured: 1.14%. Every "N× darker" claim shrank.

Lesson — the baseline is a measurement too. Every reported figure is now auto-checked against its source file.

MISTAKE 04

The champion that was a sheet of paper

The darkness leader had 0.1 mm walls standing 50 mm tall — unmanufacturable. The spreadsheet never knew; the render did.

Lesson — an optimiser always walks to the edge of the box you give it. Draw the box (manufacturing limits) honestly first.

MISTAKE 05

A paint model wrong even in its direction

Checked against the published measurement, our paint assumption was 2× optimistic head-on, 7× at grazing — and inverted the ranking.

Lesson — pin material assumptions to measured literature. This correction gave birth to the locks and gate checks.

MISTAKE 06

The honeycomb that was 30% holes

Mis-stitched hexagons left 30% of the panel as open channels to the backing. Numbers and render both looked fine at a glance. A person browsing the gallery caught it.

Lesson — "cells must tessellate" is now asserted by the code, not by people. One conclusion flipped.

MISTAKE 07

The reference plate stood inside the panel

The control plate that must always read 5.000% read low. It had been standing inside the measured panel's own field.

Lesson — contaminate the reference and every ratio is contaminated. The control value is now verified on every run.

MISTAKE 08

Three families built inside-out

Three structure families had inverted face orientations and read up to 63% too bright. Found after executing seven hypotheses in a row.

Lesson — fixing it revealed the true champion (the pyramid). Face orientation is now enforced at every builder's exit.

MISTAKE 09

We were measuring one plane

All measurements ran in the panel's frontal plane. Rotating the panel exposed a direction 74% brighter at 30° around the compass.

Lesson — the worst case is defined over all directions. The simulator now measures three planes by default.

MISTAKE 10

Four retractions, and the rule they bought

During one stretch mid-project, four published conclusions were withdrawn. Not one fell to new physics — all four were setup errors, and every one was first caught by someone else: a reviewer, a questioning colleague, the client.

Lesson — "Validate before you measure. An unvalidated sweep is worse than none, because someone will act on its ranking." That sentence became the project's first rule.

The Instrument

The instrument this journey built

When research ends, its knowledge tends to be buried in reports. So the entire study was folded into a single tool.

Pick a structure in a web browser, move the dimensions, and the exact same measurement code the study ran re-measures all three axes on the spot. The published numbers and the on-screen numbers cannot diverge, because they are produced by the same functions.

Glossary

Glossary

The words used in this report, in plain language.

Spill
The light left over after drawing, flying on until it hits a wall. The thing this study exists to erase.
Total returntotal reflectance
The share of light entering the panel that comes back into the room. 100% is a mirror; 0% is perfect darkness. Most percentages in this report are this.
Shape destructionsmear
How much of the original line shape the returned light has lost, as a multiple of a flat plate with the same paint. Higher means the picture doesn't survive.
Head-on glint
The brightest spot fired straight back at the light source, with a flat matte plate as 1. Lower is better.
Render / renderer
Building light and objects inside a computer and taking a photograph, and the calculator that does it. We use a film-industry physical renderer (Cycles) as a measuring instrument.
Ray tracing
Following one ray of light bounce by bounce. The heart of the renderer.
Specular / diffuse
Mirror-like reflection in one direction versus matte scattering in all directions. Real paint is a blend of both.
Incidence angle θ
How many degrees off the panel's normal the light arrives. 0° is head-on; 70° is a grazing strike.
Azimuth φ
Which o'clock direction the light comes from, seen face-on. Rotating the panel while measuring opens this axis.
Pitch · depth · tip
The width of one pyramid cell, the depth of its valley, and the flat left at the apex. The order spec is 4 mm · 22 mm · 0.4 mm.
Aspect ratio
Depth divided by pitch. The one geometric variable that ruled darkness in this study.
Musou Black
A commercial ultra-black water-based paint, about 1% reflectance head-on. Our paint model is pinned to a published laboratory measurement of it.
Control
An object whose answer is known in advance. A 5% matte plate stands beside every measurement, and must read 5.000% every time.
Sweep
Stepping one variable through dozens or hundreds of automated measurements. This study is the sum of several hundred sweeps.
Pre-registration
Writing down the expected answer and the pass/fail rule before running the measurement, so the story cannot be bent to fit the result.
Retroreflection
Reflection that returns light along the direction it came from. Road signs want it; we avoid it. Concave right-angle corners have it.
Coupon
A small physical test piece used to compare simulation predictions against reality before ordering at scale.
STL
The standard solid-geometry file that 3D printers and mould shops read. One button in the simulator.
