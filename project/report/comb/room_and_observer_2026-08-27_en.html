2026-08-27 · Room and observer

The room changed the answer

Nothing in this room ever hits the panel head-on. And until today this project never measured where the audience is standing. The camera was welded to the panel normal.

Verdict

  • The panel is struck at 30 to 45 degrees. Head-on never happens here. Every head-on figure published so far is a number this room does not use.
  • The honeycomb is bright when you look from the side the light came from. Not a narrow spike: it sits flat near 0.135 from 20 degrees to 60, and much of the audience stands on that side of the floor.
  • The pyramid throws it straight back at 40 degrees. 0.4675 there, 3.4 times the honeycomb, at the very angle this room uses. Move out to 60 degrees and it drops to half the honeycomb.
  • Neither one clears the target. Against a target of 0.040, on the projector's side of the floor the honeycomb is 3.4 times brighter and the pyramid 11.7 times.
  • Smaller cells win. At depth 40 the 9.53 cell beats 15 and 20 at every angle.

Why only one side is bright

A beam arriving at 40 degrees lights one wall of the cell down to 11.4 mm from the mouth. That wall's normal is perpendicular to the tube axis, so what you see depends on where you stand.

The arithmetic closes exactly. Looking up at 40 degrees from the bottom of the lit strip at 11.4 mm travels 9.53 mm sideways, which is the cell width. So the whole lit strip is visible from that direction and no other.

Where the audience stands changes the answer

The rig's camera was welded to the panel normal. setup_camera took an elevation from the start, but run_case passed a literal zero. So every peak this project has published is brightness straight out of the panel, whatever angle the beam arrived at.

Today the observer angle became a control and the same panel was viewed from several places. The sign matters. The lamp and the camera are placed by literally the same expression, so beam +40 with observer +40 looks back along the incoming light, and beam -40 with observer +40 is the mirror direction.

The honeycomb is flat and the pyramid is peaked. The pyramid throws light back at 40 degrees and falls away fast on either side. With base 50 and height 250 the faces stand at 84 degrees from the base, so two opposing faces form a narrow groove and a double bounce sends light back the way it came.

That is the choice. If the audience stands near the projector the honeycomb is better; if they stand far off and look up at 60 degrees the pyramid is better. Where the audience stands decides the shape.

What was fixed in the rig today

  • The observer angle became a control. form_buildable.OBS_ELEV, obs_elev on /api/form, a field in the UI, and the camera drawn into the 3D view. Omit it and the camera sits on the panel normal, so published figures do not move (gate_observer_angle.py, 4 items).
  • Viewed at a tilt the vertical axis compresses by cos. The mm-per-pixel on that axis is now divided by cos. Without it every oblique smear would have read too narrow.
  • The peak form returned was always the zero degree entry. There was no way to ask for the peak of a 40 degree beam. peak_by_theta was added.
  • /api/measure fell back to the withdrawn diffuse fraction 0.76 and the withdrawn roughness 0.30. Numbers 25 % wrong came out quietly. /api/rays did the same. All four slots are fixed and gate_api_defaults.py holds them. The UI and the batch scripts always sent explicit values, so they were safe.
  • A report builder now refuses to publish when a format marker is left unresolved. A doubled percent sign or a vanished paragraph slipped through three times, and neither the number checks nor the tag checks could see them.

Not measured

  • Nothing off to the side. The observer scan moved within one cross-section. The room is two-dimensional.
  • No observer scan for beams arriving at 30 or 45 degrees. Only 40.
  • Cell 6.35 at 30 to 45 degrees. Smaller cells won, so it may do better still.
  • Pyramid smear cannot be trusted on this 200 mm panel. The return overflows the window. It is left out of the table.
  • How visible any of this is to a human eye. These are all ratios.
